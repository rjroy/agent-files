# Theory: Dialogue and Voice

## Purpose

This module provides principles for writing authentic dialogue, establishing character voice through speech, using subtext effectively, and balancing dialogue with action. Use during **Phase 3: Writing Execution** alongside theory-prose-craft to bring characters to life through what they say and how they say it.

---

## Core Principles of Dialogue

### 1. Dialogue Reveals Character

**Principle:** What characters say (and how they say it) should reveal who they are.

**Dialogue Reveals:**

**Personality**
- Optimist vs. pessimist framing
- Formal vs. casual register
- Direct vs. evasive communication
- Humor style (sarcastic, earnest, dark, playful)

**Background**
- Education level (vocabulary, grammar)
- Cultural context (idioms, references)
- Profession (technical jargon, specialized knowledge)
- Regional dialect (sparingly)

**Emotional State**
- Calm: complete sentences, thoughtful pauses
- Angry: clipped words, interruptions, sharp tone
- Nervous: rambling, incomplete thoughts, hedging
- Confident: declarative statements, minimal qualifiers

**Power Dynamics**
- Who interrupts whom
- Who asks vs. commands
- Formal vs. informal address
- Length of responses (brief vs. expansive)

**Example (Telvaren):**

**Memory Shaper (precise, careful):**
```
"The memory you're describing—it's not edited. It's fabricated.
There's a difference."
```

**Resistance Fighter (blunt, action-focused):**
```
"Edited, fabricated—who cares? Someone's lying. We stop them."
```

**Scholar (analytical, historical):**
```
"This pattern resembles the Memory Wars. When consensus reality
fractures, violence follows. Always has."
```

---

### 2. Dialogue Advances Plot or Deepens Character

**Principle:** Every line should serve a purpose. Cut dialogue that doesn't work.

**Dialogue Functions:**

**Reveals Information**
- Exposition through natural conversation
- Secrets disclosed or hinted at
- Clues provided (mystery)
- World-building through character perspective

**Creates Conflict**
- Characters want different things
- Disagreement escalates tension
- Misunderstanding creates complications
- Arguments reveal deeper issues

**Develops Relationships**
- Intimacy through shared understanding
- Distance through formality or evasion
- Trust built or broken
- Alliances formed or fractured

**Shows Character Growth**
- How character speaks changes over story
- What they're willing to say evolves
- Who they can talk to shifts

**Avoid:**
- Small talk that adds nothing
- Exposition disguised as dialogue ("As you know, Bob...")
- Repetitive conversations
- Dialogue used only for pacing (use action instead)

---

### 3. Subtext - What's Not Said

**Principle:** The most important part of dialogue is often what's underneath.

**Subtext Techniques:**

**Say One Thing, Mean Another**
```
"I'm fine." [Said while crying = clearly not fine]
"That's... interesting." [Polite rejection of idea]
"You would know." [Accusation masked as statement]
```

**Avoidance**
- Character deflects from uncomfortable topic
- Changes subject abruptly
- Answers different question than asked
- Goes silent at key moment

**Contradiction Between Words and Action**
```
"I'm not worried about her."
He checked his phone for the third time in five minutes.
```

**Cultural/Social Coding**
- What can't be said directly due to social rules
- Implications understood by both speakers
- Outsider POV character might miss subtext

**Example (Telvaren Romance with Subtext):**
```
"Do you remember the first time we met?" she asked.
He was quiet too long. "Of course."
"Tell me about it."
"Why?"
She didn't answer. They both knew why. If their memories didn't
match, one of them had been edited.
```

**The subtext:** This isn't nostalgia; it's a trust test.

---

### 4. Dialogue Tags and Action Beats

**Dialogue Tags:**

**Basic Tags:**
- "Said" is invisible (use freely)
- "Asked" for questions (occasional)
- Avoid fancy tags ("exclaimed," "retorted," "interjected")
- Never use adverbs if dialogue conveys tone ("said angrily")

**Good:**
```
"I don't believe you," she said.
```

**Bad:**
```
"I don't believe you," she said disbelievingly.
```

**When to Skip Tags:**
- Two-person conversation (clear who's speaking)
- Character voice is distinctive enough
- Action beat identifies speaker
- Rhythm flows better without

**Action Beats:**

Action beats are character actions interspersed with dialogue.

**Uses:**
- Identify speaker without tag
- Show emotional state physically
- Break up long dialogue blocks
- Add pacing variation

**Example:**
```
She set down her cup. "You were there the night it happened."
"I don't remember." He wouldn't meet her eyes.
"You don't remember, or you've been edited?"
"Does it matter?"
```

**Balance:**
- Don't overuse action beats (feels choppy)
- Don't underuse (floating heads problem)
- Vary placement (before, middle, or after dialogue)

## Dialogue Craft Techniques

### 1. Natural Speech Patterns

**Real Speech Is:**

**Incomplete**
- Sentence fragments
- Trailing off mid-thought
- Interrupted sentences

**Example:**
```
"I was thinking we could—"
"No."
"You don't even know what I—"
"I said no."
```

**Repetitive**
- People repeat for emphasis
- Restate when flustered
- Return to key phrases

**Example:**
```
"It wasn't me. I swear it wasn't me. Why would I—it wasn't me."
```

**Messy**
- Filler words (um, uh, well, so)
- False starts
- Self-correction

**Use sparingly:** Too much realism becomes unreadable.

**Example (Balanced):**
```
"Well, I mean—look, I didn't see anything. Not really."
```

**Avoid:**
- Phonetic dialect ("Ah reckon y'all should...")
- Excessive stuttering
- Too many filler words
- Perfect eloquence unless character warrants it

---

### 2. Distinctive Character Voice

**Make each character sound different:**

**Vocabulary:**
- Educated character: complex words, complete sentences
- Working-class character: practical words, informal grammar
- Young character: current slang, uncertain tone
- Old character: dated references, formal structure

**Sentence Structure:**
- Short, clipped vs. long, flowing
- Questions vs. statements
- Commands vs. suggestions

**Speech Patterns:**
- Repeats certain phrases ("you know," "basically," "listen")
- Uses metaphors from their profession/background
- Formal vs. casual address ("you" vs. "ya")

**Test:** Remove dialogue tags. Can you tell who's speaking? If not, voices aren't distinct enough.

**Example (Telvaren Characters):**

**Memory Shaper (careful, precise):**
```
"The distinction matters. Editing reshapes existing memory.
Fabrication creates from nothing. Different techniques,
different ethical implications."
```

**Street Kid (informal, quick):**
```
"Yeah, yeah, editing, making up—same thing. Someone's
messing with our heads either way."
```

---

### 3. Dialogue Pacing

**Rapid-Fire (Tension, Urgency):**
- Short exchanges
- Minimal tags/beats
- Interruptions
- Incomplete sentences

**Example:**
```
"Where is it?"
"I don't—"
"Where?"
"Gone. It's gone."
"When?"
"This morning."
```

**Slower (Contemplation, Intimacy):**
- Longer speeches
- Pauses and silences
- Action beats between lines
- Complete thoughts

**Example:**
```
She was quiet for a long moment, watching the rain streak the window.
"Do you ever wonder," she said, "if we're remembering the same things
when we talk about the past? Or if we're just... telling each other
stories we both want to believe?"
He didn't answer right away. What could he say? In Telvaren, that
wasn't philosophy. It was geology—the ground shifting beneath them.
```

---

### 4. Exposition Through Dialogue

**Problem:** Dialogue can feel unnatural when delivering information.

**Bad Exposition:**
```
"As you know, Bob, the Memory Wars began fifty years ago when
the first Memory Shapers discovered how to edit collective reality."
```

**Why it's bad:** Characters don't explain what both already know.

**Good Exposition Techniques:**

**Conflict Over Information:**
```
"The Memory Wars started because of people like you."
"That's not what happened."
"Isn't it? Shapers editing reality to suit themselves?"
"We were trying to heal trauma. The government weaponized it."
```

**Outsider Needs Explanation:**
```
"What's a Memory Shaper?"
"Someone who can edit memories. Not just their own—anyone's."
"That's... that's not possible."
"Welcome to Telvaren."
```

**Information Revealed Through Action:**
```
She handed him the file. "Read page three."
His face went pale. "This says I was there. I wasn't—I don't remember—"
"That's the point. Someone made sure you wouldn't."
```

**Partial Knowledge:**
```
"I thought Shapers could only edit individual memories."
"Usually, yes. But if you synchronize enough people..."
"You can change consensus reality."
"Now you're catching on."
```

## Dialogue Quality Checklist (Phase 3)

### Character Voice

✓ Each major character sounds distinct
✓ Vocabulary matches background/education
✓ Speech patterns reflect personality
✓ Voice stays consistent throughout story
✓ Can identify speaker without tags (test it)

### Dialogue Function

✓ Every exchange serves purpose (plot, character, or relationship)
✓ No unnecessary small talk
✓ Information revealed naturally (not explained artificially)
✓ Subtext present in important conversations
✓ Dialogue advances story, doesn't pause it

### Technical Craft

✓ "Said" used freely; fancy tags avoided
✓ Action beats balance dialogue (not too many, not too few)
✓ Tags omitted when speaker is clear
✓ Adverbs on dialogue tags eliminated
✓ Natural speech patterns (fragments, interruptions) used appropriately

### Pacing

✓ Rapid exchanges for tension
✓ Slower exchanges for reflection/intimacy
✓ Rhythm varies throughout scene
✓ Dialogue blocks broken by action/interiority
✓ White space on page (not wall of text)

### Subtext

✓ Important conversations have layers
✓ Characters avoid saying key things directly (when appropriate)
✓ Actions contradict words (revealing truth)
✓ Readers can infer meaning beyond surface

## Common Dialogue Problems

### Problem: On-the-Nose Dialogue

**Symptom:** Characters say exactly what they mean and feel

**Example:**
```
"I'm angry at you because you betrayed my trust."
"I feel guilty about that."
```

**Solution:** Add indirection, let action/context show feeling
```
"You should go."
"I can explain—"
"I said go."
```

### Problem: All Characters Sound the Same

**Symptom:** Could swap speakers without changing meaning

**Solution:**
- Give each character distinctive speech patterns
- Match vocabulary to background
- Vary sentence structure per character
- Use character-specific verbal tics

### Problem: Exposition Dump

**Symptom:** Characters explain things unnaturally

**Example:**
```
"As we both know, the treaty of 2157 established three zones."
```

**Solution:**
- Reveal information through conflict over it
- Use outsider character who needs explanation
- Show information through action/discovery
- Break into smaller pieces across scenes

### Problem: Floating Heads

**Symptom:** Pages of dialogue with no action, setting, or interiority

**Solution:**
- Add action beats (character movement, gestures)
- Include sensory details of environment
- Show character's internal reactions
- Break long exchanges into scenes with movement

### Problem: Overwritten Tags/Beats

**Symptom:** Every line has elaborate action or fancy tag

**Example:**
```
"I don't know," she said uncertainly, biting her lip nervously
while fidgeting with her hair and looking away anxiously.
```

**Solution:**
- Use "said" for most tags
- One action beat per dialogue line (max)
- Let dialogue itself convey emotion
- Trust reader to infer tone

## Format-Specific Dialogue Guidance

### Flash Fiction (100-1000 words)

- Dialogue must be hyper-efficient
- Every line reveals character or advances plot
- Subtext carries heavy weight
- May have only one conversation
- Voice must be immediately distinctive

### Short Story (1000-5000 words)

- Room for 2-3 substantial conversations
- Can develop relationship through dialogue arc
- Balance dialogue with action/description
- Key scenes should have dialogue centerpieces
- Voice can develop subtly

### Epic (10,000+ words)

- Multiple extended conversations
- Dialogue style can vary by scene type
- Character voice can evolve over story
- Relationship dynamics shift through dialogue
- Room for both rapid exchanges and lengthy discussions

### Serialized (Chapter-based)

- Each chapter might have distinct dialogue rhythm
- Recurring phrases create character continuity
- Dialogue can callback to previous chapters
- Character speech patterns should stay consistent across installments

## Special Dialogue Scenarios

### Argument/Conflict Dialogue

**Techniques:**
- Interruptions (characters talk over each other)
- Incomplete sentences (too angry/flustered to finish)
- Volume shifts (caps for yelling, used sparingly)
- Repetition (stuck on key point)
- Short, sharp exchanges

**Example:**
```
"You knew. The whole time, you—"
"I was protecting you."
"Protecting me? You lied to—"
"I didn't lie."
"You didn't tell the truth!"
```

### Intimate/Romantic Dialogue

**Techniques:**
- Subtext (what's not said matters more)
- Pauses and silences
- Physical awareness woven through
- Vulnerability shown through small admissions
- Avoid clichés ("You complete me")

**Example:**
```
"Stay," she said.
He should go. Should say no. Should—
"Okay."
```

### Group Conversations (3+ People)

**Challenges:**
- Hard to track who's speaking
- Can feel chaotic

**Techniques:**
- Use clear tags/beats for first few exchanges
- Give each speaker distinctive voice
- Have characters address each other by name
- Break into smaller sub-conversations
- Use action beats to show speaker positions

### Internal Dialogue (Character Thoughts)

**Format Options:**
- Italics (traditional)
- Regular text, no quotes (modern)
- Clearly marked as thought through context

**Use for:**
- Character's true feelings (vs. what they say)
- Internal conflict
- Processing information
- Plan formulation

**Avoid:**
- Overuse (pulls reader from scene)
- Redundancy (thought + dialogue saying same thing)

## Dialogue in World-Building (Telvaren Example)

**Dialogue Can Reveal World Mechanics:**

**Through Character Reactions:**
```
"I changed my mind about dinner."
She stiffened. "When?"
"This morning. Why?"
"Just asking." But her hand went to her temple—the unconscious
gesture of someone checking their memories.
```

**Through Casual References:**
```
"We need to sync our story before the Shaper arrives."
"I hate this. Lying in advance."
"It's not lying if we both believe it afterward."
```

**Through Conflict Over World Elements:**
```
"Editing trauma memories isn't murder."
"You're killing who they were."
"I'm saving who they can become."
```

**Principle:** World-building through dialogue feels natural when characters disagree about it.

## Final Principles

1. **Every line serves a purpose** - Plot, character, or relationship
2. **Subtext > text** - What's unsaid is often more important
3. **Voice distinguishes** - Each character should sound like themselves
4. **"Said" is invisible** - Don't fight it with fancy tags
5. **Action grounds dialogue** - Balance talking heads with movement
6. **Natural ≠ realistic** - Clean up real speech, don't transcribe it
7. **Conflict reveals** - Disagreement shows character depth
8. **Silence speaks** - What characters won't say matters

This theory module grounds dialogue authenticity. Apply it in Phase 3 alongside theory-prose-craft to create conversations that feel real, reveal character, and advance your story.
