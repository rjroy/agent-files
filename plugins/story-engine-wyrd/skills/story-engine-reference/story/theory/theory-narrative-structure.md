# Theory: Narrative Structure

## Purpose

This module provides foundational principles for plot construction, conflict development, tension management, and pacing. Use during **Phase 1: Story Concept Development** to build structurally sound narrative foundations.

---

## Core Elements of Narrative Structure

### 1. Conflict - The Engine of Story

**Principle:** Every story needs tension between what a character wants and what prevents them from getting it.

**Types of Conflict:**

**External Conflict** - Character vs. outside forces
- Character vs. Character (interpersonal)
- Character vs. Society (systemic)
- Character vs. Nature/World (environmental)
- Character vs. Technology/System (mechanical)

**Internal Conflict** - Character vs. self
- Desire vs. Fear
- Duty vs. Want
- Identity vs. Expectation
- Belief vs. Reality

**Best Practice:** Layer external and internal conflicts
- External conflict provides plot momentum
- Internal conflict provides emotional depth
- They should complicate each other

**Example (Telvaren):**
- External: Protagonist must expose a dangerous memory edit
- Internal: Protagonist fears they might have been memory-edited themselves
- Complication: Exposing the truth requires trusting their own memories

---

### 2. Plot Structure - The Shape of Events

**Three-Act Structure (Classic)**

**Act 1: Setup (25%)**
- Establish world, character, status quo
- Inciting incident disrupts normalcy
- Character commits to goal/journey
- Stakes are established

**Act 2: Confrontation (50%)**
- Character pursues goal, faces obstacles
- Complications escalate
- Midpoint: Major revelation or reversal
- Darkest moment: All seems lost

**Act 3: Resolution (25%)**
- Climax: Final confrontation
- Resolution: Consequences play out
- New equilibrium established

**Alternative Structures:**

**Kishotenketsu (East Asian four-act)**
- Introduction → Development → Twist → Reconciliation
- Emphasizes connection over conflict
- Good for contemplative or thematic stories

**In Medias Res**
- Start in the middle of action
- Reveal context through flashbacks/exposition
- Good for mysteries and thrillers

**Circular Structure**
- End where you began, but transformed
- Emphasizes change through comparison
- Good for character studies

**Choose structure based on:**
- Story format (flash needs tight focus, epic allows complexity)
- World mechanics (memory-shifting might demand non-linear structure)
- Thematic goals (what truth are you exploring?)

---

### 3. Tension - The Promise of Consequence

**Principle:** Tension is the reader's anticipation that something important is at stake.

**Building Tension:**

**Raise Stakes**
- Make consequences increasingly severe
- Connect conflict to what character values most
- Threaten multiple levels (physical, emotional, moral)

**Create Urgency**
- Add time pressure (deadline, countdown)
- Make inaction as dangerous as action
- Force decisions before character feels ready

**Complicate Choices**
- No purely good options
- Each solution creates new problems
- Force character to sacrifice something important

**Withhold Information**
- Create questions readers need answered
- Promise revelations without delivering immediately
- Use dramatic irony (reader knows more than character, or vice versa)

**Escalate Obstacles**
- Each obstacle should be harder than the last
- Character's solutions should stop working
- Force character to change tactics/grow

**Example (Mystery Lens + Telvaren):**
- Stakes: If the edited memory isn't exposed, innocent people suffer
- Urgency: The memory is being reinforced; soon it'll be "true" for everyone
- Complication: Exposing it requires trusting a Memory Shaper who might be corrupt
- Withholding: The protagonist doesn't know if their own memories are reliable
- Escalation: Every investigation attempt gets them memory-edited in small ways

---

### 4. Pacing - The Rhythm of Revelation

**Principle:** Vary the speed at which information is revealed and events unfold.

**Fast Pacing (Action/Urgency):**
- Short sentences and paragraphs
- Active verbs, minimal description
- Dialogue without extensive tags
- Events compressed in time
- Use for: chases, fights, critical decisions, revelations

**Slow Pacing (Reflection/Atmosphere):**
- Longer sentences, detailed description
- Sensory immersion
- Character interiority and contemplation
- Events extended in time
- Use for: world-building, character development, emotional processing

**Pacing Rhythm:**
- Alternate fast and slow sections
- Earn slow moments with preceding tension
- Use fast moments to propel toward slow revelations
- End scenes/chapters on accelerating pace (hooks)

**Format Considerations:**
- **Flash Fiction:** Mostly fast, one slow moment for resonance
- **Short Story:** Build to climax, then brief denouement
- **Epic:** Vary significantly; multiple peaks and valleys
- **Serialized:** Each chapter needs its own pacing arc + series arc

---

### 5. Scene vs. Summary

**Scene** - Show events in real-time
- Dialogue, action, sensory detail
- Reader experiences alongside character
- Use for: important moments, emotional beats, conflict

**Summary** - Tell what happened
- Compressed time, reported events
- Reader learns information efficiently
- Use for: transitions, necessary background, less important events

**Balance:**
- Scene = dramatic weight
- Summary = narrative efficiency
- Don't scene everything; don't summarize what matters

## Story Concept Development Checklist

When developing a story outline in Phase 1, ensure you have:

### Conflict Clarity
✓ What does the protagonist want?
✓ What prevents them from getting it?
✓ What internal conflict complicates the external one?
✓ How do world mechanics create or intensify conflict?

### Structural Foundation
✓ What structure best serves this story's needs?
✓ Where does the story begin? (Status quo or in medias res?)
✓ What's the inciting incident that disrupts normalcy?
✓ What's the midpoint revelation or reversal?
✓ What's the climactic confrontation?
✓ How does the story resolve?

### Tension Architecture
✓ What are the stakes? (What happens if protagonist fails?)
✓ What creates urgency? (Why now? What forces action?)
✓ What complicates choices? (Why is this difficult?)
✓ What information is withheld? (What questions drive the reader?)
✓ How do obstacles escalate? (How does it get worse?)

### Pacing Plan
✓ Where are the fast sections? (Action, urgency, revelation)
✓ Where are the slow sections? (Reflection, atmosphere, depth)
✓ How do you vary rhythm?
✓ What's the pacing arc for the format? (Flash vs. epic needs)

### World Integration
✓ How do world mechanics drive the plot?
✓ How does the world create unique obstacles?
✓ How does the world enable unique solutions?
✓ Is the world integral or just decorative?

### Lens Application (if applicable)
✓ How does the lens reframe world mechanics?
✓ Does the lens enhance tension or reveal new conflicts?
✓ Are genre conventions serving the story or constraining it?

## Common Structural Problems

### Problem: Sagging Middle

**Symptom:** Act 2 feels aimless or repetitive

**Solutions:**
- Add midpoint reversal that changes the character's approach
- Escalate obstacles meaningfully (don't just repeat the same challenge)
- Introduce subplots that complicate main conflict
- Reveal new information that recontextualizes everything

### Problem: Unearned Ending

**Symptom:** Resolution feels sudden or convenient

**Solutions:**
- Set up solutions earlier (plant seeds in Act 1/2)
- Make character earn victory through growth or sacrifice
- Ensure climax directly confronts the core conflict
- Avoid deus ex machina (external force solving everything)

### Problem: Weak Stakes

**Symptom:** Reader doesn't care if protagonist succeeds

**Solutions:**
- Connect conflict to character's deepest values
- Show what will be lost (make it specific and personal)
- Demonstrate consequences through smaller failures first
- Make stakes emotional, not just physical

### Problem: Predictable Plot

**Symptom:** Reader sees every twist coming

**Solutions:**
- Subvert genre expectations (then deliver on them differently)
- Give character surprising choices that reveal complexity
- Use misdirection (hint at one outcome, deliver another)
- Complicate what "success" means (winning ≠ happy ending)

## Format-Specific Structural Guidance

### Flash Fiction (100-1000 words)

**Structure:** Single scene or moment
- No room for complex plot
- Focus on one revelation, transformation, or realization
- In medias res opening
- Implication over explanation
- Ending resonates through what's unsaid

**Example Arc:**
1. Drop into charged moment
2. Reveal one key detail that recontextualizes
3. End with shift in understanding or reality

### Short Story (1000-5000 words)

**Structure:** Tight three-act or focused arc
- One protagonist, one primary conflict
- 2-4 key scenes (setup, complication, climax, resolution)
- Limited time span (days, not years)
- Focused scope (intimate, not epic)
- Thematic clarity

**Example Arc:**
1. Establish character and desire
2. Introduce obstacle and complication
3. Force decision or confrontation
4. Resolve with change or revelation

### Epic (10,000+ words)

**Structure:** Multi-act with subplots
- Multiple POV characters possible
- Parallel storylines that converge
- Extended time span
- Multiple conflicts operating simultaneously
- Complex world-building woven through plot
- Chapters as structural units

**Example Arc:**
1. Establish world and stakes broadly
2. Interweave character arcs
3. Build toward convergence
4. Multiple climaxes across storylines
5. Resolve on thematic and plot levels

### Serialized (Chapter-based)

**Structure:** Episodic with throughline
- Each chapter has mini-arc (setup/payoff)
- Chapter-end hooks create urgency
- Overall arc builds across installments
- Character development is gradual
- Recurring elements create coherence

**Example Chapter Arc:**
1. Resolve or advance previous cliffhanger
2. Develop current episode's conflict
3. Advance overall series arc
4. End on hook for next chapter

## Final Principles

1. **Structure serves story, not vice versa** - Adapt frameworks to your needs
2. **Conflict must be personal** - Abstract stakes don't engage readers
3. **Tension requires plausible threat** - If failure isn't believable, stakes collapse
4. **Pacing is emotional rhythm** - Fast/slow mirrors urgency/reflection cycles
5. **World mechanics should create unique structural opportunities** - Use what makes your world special

This theory module grounds narrative architecture. Apply it in Phase 1 to create story outlines that are structurally sound and ready for character development and prose execution.
