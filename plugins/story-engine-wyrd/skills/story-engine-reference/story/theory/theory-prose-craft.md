# Theory: Prose Craft

## Purpose

This module provides principles for writing effective, evocative prose: description, style, voice, pacing through sentence structure, and sensory immersion. Use during **Phase 3: Writing Execution** to transform story concepts and character plans into compelling narrative prose.

---

## Core Principles of Prose

### 1. Show vs. Tell - Immersion Through Specificity

**Principle:** Let readers experience through concrete details, not abstract statements.

**Tell (Abstract):**
- "She was angry."
- "The city was dangerous."
- "He felt afraid."

**Show (Concrete):**
- "She slammed the cup down, coffee splattering across the table."
- "Three buildings on this street alone had bars over their windows."
- "His hand shook as he reached for the door handle."

**When to Tell:**
- Transitional information (low importance)
- Background that doesn't need full scene
- Pacing acceleration (skip unimportant moments)

**When to Show:**
- Emotional beats (character feelings)
- Important character revealing moments
- Key plot events
- World-building that creates atmosphere

**Balance:** Show what matters, tell what connects.

---

### 2. Sensory Detail - Building Lived-In Worlds

**Principle:** Readers experience through senses. Use all five.

**The Five Senses:**

**Sight (Most Used)**
- Color, light, shadow, movement
- Avoid generic description ("beautiful," "ugly")
- Be specific: "rust-colored" not "reddish," "fluorescent glare" not "bright light"

**Sound**
- Ambient noise creates atmosphere
- Silence can be as important as sound
- Describe quality: harsh, soft, rhythmic, chaotic

**Smell**
- Powerfully evocative (triggers memory)
- Often neglected but highly immersive
- Can establish setting quickly

**Touch**
- Texture, temperature, pressure
- Internal sensations (muscle tension, heartbeat, breath)
- Physical grounding for emotional states

**Taste**
- Less frequent but memorable when used
- Good for food scenes, intimate moments
- Can reveal character (what they notice tasting)

**Sensory Balance:**
- Don't dump all five senses at once
- Layer senses naturally through scene
- Match sensory focus to POV character (what would *they* notice?)

**Example (Telvaren):**
```
The memory archive smelled of old paper and something metallic—like blood,
but older. Colder. She ran her fingers along the shelf, dust gritting
beneath her nails. In the silence, her heartbeat felt too loud, as if
the room were listening.
```

### 3. Sentence Structure - Rhythm and Pacing

**Principle:** Sentence length and structure create emotional rhythm.

**Short Sentences:**
- Fast pacing
- Urgency, tension
- Action, decisive moments
- Clarity, emphasis

**Example:**
```
She ran. Behind her, footsteps. Closer. She turned left. Dead end.
```

**Long Sentences:**
- Slower pacing
- Reflection, contemplation
- Building complexity or atmosphere
- Flowing, dreamlike, or overwhelming feeling

**Example:**
```
Memory fragments drifted through her mind like snow—each one crystalline,
perfect, and she couldn't tell which were real and which were planted,
couldn't trust even her own childhood because memory was currency here and
someone had been spending hers.
```

**Varied Sentences (Best Practice):**
- Alternate short and long for rhythm
- Match structure to emotional beat
- Use fragments for emphasis (sparingly)
- Break rules intentionally for effect

**Paragraph Length:**
- Short paragraphs = fast pacing, urgency
- Long paragraphs = immersion, slow contemplation
- Single-sentence paragraphs = dramatic emphasis
- Vary to create visual rhythm on page

---

### 4. Word Choice - Precision and Connotation

**Principle:** Every word carries both denotation (meaning) and connotation (feeling).

**Active vs. Passive Voice:**

**Passive (Weak):**
- "The memory was edited by someone."
- "The door was opened."

**Active (Strong):**
- "Someone edited the memory."
- "She opened the door."

**When to use passive:**
- When actor is unknown or unimportant
- To emphasize what was done over who did it
- For variety in rhythm (occasional)

**Verb Strength:**

**Weak Verbs:**
- "Walked quickly" → **Sprinted**
- "Looked carefully" → **Scrutinized**
- "Said loudly" → **Shouted**

**Avoid:**
- Adverbs when stronger verbs exist
- Generic verbs + adverb combos
- "Very" as intensifier (find stronger word)

**Specific Nouns:**
- "Tree" → "Oak," "Willow," "Pine"
- "Flower" → "Rose," "Dandelion," "Orchid"
- "Building" → "Warehouse," "Cathedral," "Tenement"

**Precision = Immersion:** Specific words create clearer mental images.

---

### 5. Point of View - Filtering Reality

**Principle:** Everything readers experience is filtered through POV character's perspective.

**First Person ("I"):**
- Intimate, immediate
- Reader in character's head
- Limited to character's knowledge
- Strong voice essential

**Third Person Limited ("She"):**
- Close to character but slight distance
- Can describe character externally
- Limited to one character's knowledge per scene
- Most flexible for fiction

**Third Person Omniscient ("The woman"):**
- Narrator knows all
- Can jump between characters
- Can comment on action
- Harder to maintain intimacy

**Best Practice for Story Engine:**
- **First person:** Flash fiction, short stories (intimate connection)
- **Third limited:** Short stories, epics (flexibility + intimacy)
- **Third omniscient:** Epics with multiple storylines (scope)

**POV Filtering Example:**

**Neutral:** "The room was ten feet by ten feet."

**Filtered through character:**
- Detective: "The room was small—ten by ten, no windows, one exit. A trap."
- Architect: "The room's proportions were precise: a perfect square, ten feet per side."
- Prisoner: "The room was a cage. Ten feet. She'd counted."

**Match description to character's profession, obsessions, emotional state.**

## Prose Craft Techniques

### 1. Opening Lines - Hook Immediately

**Purpose:** First sentence must pull reader in.

**Effective Openings:**

**Drop into action/tension:**
```
The memory that killed her father wasn't hers.
```

**Establish voice and world:**
```
In Telvaren, forgetting is a crime, but remembering is worse.
```

**Create immediate question:**
```
The first time she edited a memory, she swore she'd never do it again.
```

**Avoid:**
- Weather description (unless symbolically vital)
- Character waking up (cliché)
- Exposition dumps
- Generic scene-setting

### 2. Description Techniques

**Objective Correlative:**
Use external details to convey internal states.

**Example:**
Instead of "She felt isolated and trapped," show:
```
The streets folded in on themselves, every turn leading back to
the same empty square. No one walked here anymore.
```

**Pathetic Fallacy:**
Nature/environment reflects emotional state (use sparingly).

**Example:**
```
Rain hammered the windows as she read the letter, each word another
nail in the coffin of her certainty.
```

**Metaphor and Simile:**
- **Simile:** Comparison using "like" or "as" (more gentle)
  - "Memory fragments fell like snow"
- **Metaphor:** Direct comparison (more forceful)
  - "Memory fragments were snow, melting before she could grasp them"

**Use metaphors that:**
- Fit the world (Telvaren = memory/reality metaphors)
- Illuminate rather than confuse
- Don't draw attention to themselves
- Come from POV character's experience

**Avoid:**
- Mixed metaphors ("time is money" + "drowning in time")
- Clichés ("quiet as a mouse," "cold as ice")
- Over-explaining metaphors (trust reader to get it)

### 3. Scene Construction

**Scene Elements:**

**Setting** - Where/when, established quickly
**Action** - What happens (plot movement)
**Reaction** - How characters respond (character development)
**Dialogue** - What's said (relationship dynamics)

**Scene Flow:**
1. **Establish** - Ground reader in space, time, who's present
2. **Build** - Escalate tension or develop emotion
3. **Turn** - Something changes (new information, decision, action)
4. **Transition** - Lead into next scene or end chapter

**Example (Telvaren Mystery):**
```
1. Establish: She entered the Memory Archive after hours—dust, silence, forbidden.
2. Build: Found the file she wasn't supposed to see.
3. Turn: The file showed her own memories being edited.
4. Transition: She ran, not knowing who to trust.
```

### 4. Pacing Through Prose

**To Speed Up:**
- Short sentences
- Active voice
- More action, less description
- Cut interiority (character thoughts)
- More dialogue, fewer tags
- Shorter paragraphs

**To Slow Down:**
- Longer sentences
- Rich sensory detail
- More interiority (character reflection)
- Descriptive passages
- Longer paragraphs
- Deliberate, flowing rhythm

**Pacing Rhythm:**
Alternate fast and slow to create heartbeat of story.
- Fast: action, revelation, urgency
- Slow: reflection, atmosphere, emotional processing
- Then accelerate again

## Prose Quality Checklist (Phase 3 Execution)

### Opening (First 100-200 words)

✓ Immediate hook (tension, question, or voice)
✓ World established quickly (not explained, shown)
✓ POV character clear
✓ Tone/atmosphere set
✓ No exposition dumps

### Scene-Level

✓ Sensory detail creates immersion (not just sight)
✓ Show important moments, tell transitions
✓ Description filtered through POV character
✓ Pacing matches emotional beat (fast = action, slow = reflection)
✓ Each scene has purpose (plot or character development)

### Sentence-Level

✓ Varied sentence length creates rhythm
✓ Active voice dominant (passive only for effect)
✓ Strong, specific verbs (minimal adverbs)
✓ Precise nouns (avoid generic words)
✓ No clichés
✓ Metaphors fit world and character

### Paragraph-Level

✓ Varied paragraph length for visual rhythm
✓ Single-sentence paragraphs for emphasis (sparingly)
✓ Paragraph breaks for pacing and clarity
✓ Each paragraph advances scene

### Overall Prose

✓ Voice is consistent and distinctive
✓ World details integrated naturally (not explained)
✓ Emotional beats earned through buildup
✓ Reader experiences alongside character (not watching from distance)
✓ Prose serves story (not showing off)

## Common Prose Problems

### Problem: Purple Prose (Overwritten)

**Symptom:** Every sentence is flowery, metaphorical, or ornate

**Example:**
```
The luminescent orb of celestial fire descended languorously beyond
the verdant horizon, painting the empyrean vault with crimson hues.
```

**Solution:** Simplify. Clarity beats ornamentation.
```
The sun set, red bleeding across the sky.
```

**Principle:** Ornate prose for special moments, clarity for most.

### Problem: Flat Prose (Underwritten)

**Symptom:** Every sentence is bare statement, no imagery or rhythm

**Example:**
```
She walked to the door. She opened it. A man stood there. He was tall.
She let him in.
```

**Solution:** Add sensory detail, vary structure, create rhythm.
```
She crossed to the door, hesitating before the handle. When she opened it,
a tall man filled the frame—backlit, face in shadow. She stepped aside.
```

### Problem: Telling Emotions

**Symptom:** Character emotions stated directly

**Example:**
```
She was terrified. She felt hopeless.
```

**Solution:** Show through physical sensation, action, thought pattern.
```
Her hands wouldn't stop shaking. Every plan she considered fell apart
before she finished the thought.
```

### Problem: Unclear Antecedents

**Symptom:** Pronouns don't clearly refer to nouns

**Example:**
```
She told her sister she was wrong. [Who's wrong?]
```

**Solution:** Use names or restructure.
```
"You're wrong," she told her sister.
```

### Problem: Adverb Overload

**Symptom:** Too many -ly words modifying verbs

**Example:**
```
She quickly ran swiftly across the room loudly.
```

**Solution:** Use stronger verbs, cut redundancy.
```
She sprinted across the room, footsteps echoing.
```

## Format-Specific Prose Guidance

### Flash Fiction (100-1000 words)

- **Every word must earn its place**
- Strong opening hook (no room for slow build)
- Implied background (can't explain everything)
- Sensory detail creates world quickly
- Ending must resonate (readers remember last line)

### Short Story (1000-5000 words)

- Room for atmosphere and character voice
- Balance showing vs. telling (can't scene everything)
- Vary pacing (fast action, slow reflection)
- Strong scenes for key moments, summary for transitions
- Satisfying ending (not rushed)

### Epic (10,000+ words)

- Establish prose rhythm early (reader will spend time here)
- Can develop rich sensory world
- Vary style across chapters (action vs. reflection)
- Different POV characters can have subtly different prose styles
- Pacing varies significantly (mountains and valleys)

### Serialized (Chapter-based)

- Each chapter has distinct prose arc
- Chapter endings need propulsive rhythm (hooks)
- Can experiment with style across chapters
- Maintain overall voice consistency
- Readers will take breaks (remind them of atmosphere when returning)

## Final Principles

1. **Clarity first, beauty second** - Reader should never struggle to understand
2. **Sensory detail creates immersion** - Abstract statements create distance
3. **Rhythm is emotional** - Fast/slow pacing matches tension/reflection
4. **Specificity beats generality** - "Oak" beats "tree," "sprinted" beats "went fast"
5. **POV is a filter** - Everything described is how character experiences it
6. **Show what matters, tell what connects** - Not everything needs full scene
7. **Voice consistency** - Prose style should feel cohesive throughout
8. **Serve the story** - Prose exists to convey narrative, not showcase vocabulary

This theory module grounds prose execution. Apply it in Phase 3 alongside theory-dialogue-and-voice to write stories that are clear, evocative, and distinctive.
